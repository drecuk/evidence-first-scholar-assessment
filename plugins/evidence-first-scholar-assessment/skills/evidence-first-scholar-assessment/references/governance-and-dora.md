## DORA in this workflow

[DORA](https://sfdora.org/) is the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment. It calls for improving the ways research outputs and researchers are evaluated. The key operational principle here is to assess research on its own merits rather than using the journal in which it appeared—or journal-based measures—as a surrogate for quality.

Primary links:

- [Read the Declaration](https://sfdora.org/read/)
- [Guidance on the responsible use of quantitative indicators](https://sfdora.org/resource/guidance-on-the-responsible-use-of-quantitative-indicators-in-research-assessment/)
- [DORA resources](https://sfdora.org/resource-library-for-research-assessment/)

Describe this evaluator as **DORA-informed**. Do not imply endorsement, certification, partnership, or affiliation with DORA.

The practical implications are:

1. Read and judge selected outputs for originality, scholarly significance, rigour, attributable contribution, and durability.
2. Use indicators to illuminate scale, reach, recency, concentration, and anomalies—not to replace judgment.
3. State how each indicator was produced and what it cannot establish.
4. Do not infer article or scholar quality from Journal Impact Factor, quartile, publisher, institutional affiliation, or award prestige.
5. Use multiple forms of output and contribution evidence appropriate to the discipline.

## Proper and improper uses of metrics

An indicator is suitable only when it is:

- **Clear:** the reader understands what it measures.
- **Transparent:** provider, formula, numerator, denominator, window, and date are recorded.
- **Specific:** it addresses a defined assessment question.
- **Contextual:** discipline, career stage, team size, output culture, and database coverage are considered.
- **Fair:** the same rule is applied to candidate and peers, with relevant accommodations and correction opportunities.

Appropriate uses include describing citation reach, identifying a concentrated record that warrants close reading, testing whether influence is current or historical, comparing normalized indicators among genuinely similar peers, and checking whether conclusions survive alternative corpus rules.

Improper uses include automatic shortlisting by h-index, treating a journal metric as article quality, comparing raw counts across incompatible fields, using a universal threshold, converting a small peer group into a precise percentile, or presenting one provider's author profile as ground truth.

## Google Scholar profiles

Including Google Scholar data does **not** by itself break DORA principles. It becomes inconsistent with those principles when the profile is used as a shortcut for quality or as an automatic decision rule.

Use Google Scholar only when all of the following are satisfied:

1. Verify the profile using name, affiliation, verified email domain, coauthors, topics, and confirmed works.
2. Record the snapshot date and distinguish displayed profile totals from metrics recomputed on the validated corpus.
3. Check for merged namesakes, duplicate versions, omissions, and automatically accepted records.
4. Report citations, h-index, and i10-index as descriptive context. Give them no automatic points.
5. Cross-check consequential works against a second provider where practical; never add the totals together.

Self-declared interest labels require additional caution. Capture all labels, preregister which labels are relevant, save the exact label token and observation conditions, repeat when feasible, and substantiate the interest from verified outputs. Report only an observed page/row position. Google does not document these pages as complete or official field rankings, so never convert them into “top X worldwide” claims or percentiles without a stable enumerable population.

## Scope exclusions

Exclude from the core research-quality and scholarly-impact score:

- administrative titles and institutional status;
- management, fundraising, teaching, supervision, committee work, and service;
- publicity, media claims, and celebratory biographies;
- editorial roles, fellowships, keynotes, awards, or memberships unless tied to a specific output and used only as corroboration;
- grant count, total, or prestige by itself;
- industry, policy, clinical, professional, technical, or public impact unless separately requested as an external-impact annex.

Funding can be contextual evidence when the role, award, dates, amount, and links to outputs are verified. External impact must establish a chain from research output to candidate contribution, external use, change, outcome, and independent corroboration; keep it separate from the research score.

## Candidate correction and fairness

Give the candidate a defined opportunity to correct identity, corpus, contribution, and missing-data errors before finalization. Log corrections and their effects. Apply the same inclusion rules, windows, source hierarchy, and missing-data treatment to peers. State conflicts of interest, access asymmetries, career interruptions, part-time research fractions, and discipline-specific output norms where relevant.
